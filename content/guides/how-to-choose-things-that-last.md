## Introduction

The things that remain with us are rarely chosen for novelty alone. They earn their place through proportion, usefulness, material honesty, and the way they settle into everyday life.

This guide offers a simple way to look beyond the first impression and choose objects with the potential to become more personal over time.

## Chapters

### 01 / Begin with use

Start with the life an object needs to support. A chair should invite sitting, a bag should carry what is actually needed, and a table should leave enough room for the rituals that gather around it.

When the use is clear, unnecessary gestures fall away. What remains tends to be calmer, more direct, and easier to live with.

![A considered everyday object seen in use](images/akira-back.jpg)

### 02 / Read the material

Look for surfaces that can accept touch, light, wear, and repair. Natural variation is not an imperfection; it is often what allows an object to develop character rather than simply look used.

Ask how the material will change. The answer matters as much as how it appears when new.

![Material and form shaped by natural light](images/ongozisin.jpg)

### 03 / Leave room for time

A lasting object does not need to resolve everything at once. It can be quiet enough to move between rooms, routines, and stages of life without losing its purpose.

Choose slowly. Familiarity is part of the test: what continues to feel right after the first excitement has passed is often what deserves to stay.

![A restrained composition with room around it](images/maha.jpg)

## Closing

The aim is not permanence for its own sake. It is to build a relationship with fewer, better things—and to let attention become part of their value.
